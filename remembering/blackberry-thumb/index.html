He Saw Exactly Why the iPhone Would Win. He Bet Against It Anyway

The BlackBerry's tiny physical keyboard was so widely used for one-handed typing that doctors gave the resulting repetitive strain injury its own nickname: "BlackBerry thumb."

Key takeaway

People didn't put up with the BlackBerry's cramped keyboard despite the discomfort. For a few years, the discomfort was almost part of the status symbol.

What Actually Happened

Before the smartphone became a screen, it was a keyboard. BlackBerry understood this better than almost anyone. Its small QWERTY keyboard wasn't designed to disappear beneath glass. It sat permanently beneath your thumbs, letting executives, journalists, politicians and anyone who needed to stay connected type messages without looking away from the world around them.

By the 2000s, the BlackBerry had become shorthand for being reachable. Its users developed a distinctive rhythm: phone in one hand, thumb moving rapidly across tiny keys, email arriving almost continuously. The nickname came soon after. “CrackBerry” described the compulsive need to check and respond to messages. “BlackBerry thumb” described what happened when that habit was repeated often enough.

The irony was that the very thing people loved about the BlackBerry — the physical keyboard — was also what made the strain possible. But nobody was asking for it to disappear. The keyboard gave typing something a touchscreen couldn't yet replicate: physical feedback. You could feel each key. You could type without looking. You could develop muscle memory. The inconvenience had become part of the experience.

Why It Still Matters

Then the screen took over. When the iPhone arrived in 2007, Apple didn't make a better physical keyboard. It removed the keyboard altogether. The interface became software. The keys appeared only when they were needed, changing with the task. Suddenly, the thing BlackBerry had built its identity around — hundreds of tiny physical buttons under your thumbs — became unnecessary.

The habit disappeared remarkably quickly. Today, most people type on a completely flat piece of glass without thinking about what was lost. No click. No key travel. No physical keyboard. And, for most of us, no BlackBerry thumb. The BlackBerry wasn't necessarily badly designed.

It was perfectly designed for the world it belonged to. Its keyboard solved a real problem so well that millions of people were willing to live with its limitations. Then the problem changed. And the design that once made the BlackBerry indispensable became the thing that made it difficult to imagine the future.

That is why the BlackBerry is worth remembering. Sometimes a design doesn't fail because it was badly made. It fails because the world around it changes.

DelvauxHow the Steam Engine Invented the Modern Handbag

Founded in 1829, Delvaux is often cited as the oldest fine leather goods house in the world - and it built its reputation designing bags for the first generation of railway travellers.

Key takeaway

Delvaux's early handbags weren't accessories. They were a solution to a new problem: how to carry your own belongings once trains meant you no longer had a porter.

What Actually Happened

The modern handbag exists because travel changed. For centuries, people travelled with trunks. They were large, heavy and rarely carried by their owners. A journey usually meant servants, porters or horse-drawn carriages. Your belongings travelled with you, but seldom in your hands.

Then the railway arrived. Trains transformed travel during the nineteenth century. Journeys became faster, more frequent and increasingly personal. Travellers could no longer rely on someone else to manage every piece of luggage. They needed something smaller. Something practical. Something they could carry themselves.

Delvaux recognised the shift. Founded in Brussels in 1829, the house began creating finely crafted leather bags designed for this new generation of railway travellers. They were elegant, but elegance wasn't the starting point. Convenience was.

As travel evolved, so did the bag. Delvaux continually refined its designs, adapting them to changing habits while maintaining an uncompromising standard of craftsmanship. Long before authenticity became a concern across luxury, the house introduced individually numbered certificates for its creations-a quiet commitment to provenance decades ahead of its time.

Like many enduring objects, the handbag wasn't born as a fashion statement. It began as a practical response to a changing world.

Why It Still Matters

The remarkable thing isn't that Delvaux survived nearly two centuries. It's that the handbag survived one of the biggest technological shifts in history. Before the railway, people travelled with trunks. They were designed to stay closed until the journey ended.

Trains changed that. Travel became faster, more frequent and more independent. Instead of packing everything into a large trunk, people needed certain belongings within reach-tickets, documents, money, gloves, notebooks and personal effects. The journey itself had changed, and the objects people carried had to change with it.

The handbag emerged as an answer to that new way of travelling. More than a century later, the technology has changed again. Steam trains gave way to cars. Cars to airplanes. Paper tickets to smartphones. Yet the handbag remains. Nearly every major fashion house designs one, but the reason we carry it has hardly changed. It still keeps the essentials close, just as it did for the first railway travellers.

Its endurance isn't built on fashion alone. It's built on craftsmanship answering a genuine change in how people moved through the world. Sometimes new technology doesn't just change how we travel. It quietly changes the objects we can't imagine travelling without.

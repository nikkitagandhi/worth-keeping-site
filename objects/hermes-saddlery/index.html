Hermès Didn't Start With Handbags. It Started With Harnesses

When Thierry Hermès opened his workshop in Paris in 1837, he wasn't making handbags - he was making harnesses and saddles for European noblemen's carriage horses.

Key takeaway

Every Hermès bag still carries the saddle-stitching technique developed for leather that had to survive a horse, not just a shoulder.

What Actually Happened

Before Hermès carried handbags, it carried horses. When Thierry Hermès opened his Paris workshop in 1837, the world still moved at the pace of a carriage. His clients weren't looking for fashion. They needed harnesses and saddles that could withstand rain, movement and years of daily use.

Failure wasn't inconvenient. It was dangerous. Every stitch mattered. At the heart of that craftsmanship was the saddle stitch-a hand-sewn technique that locks every stitch independently, making it exceptionally strong and durable. It became one of the defining skills of the house, not because it was beautiful, but because it had to last.

Then the world changed. The automobile gradually replaced the horse, and the demand for harnesses began to disappear. For many workshops, it marked the end of an era. For Hermès, it became the beginning of another. Rather than abandoning its craft, the house carried it into every new way people travelled.

In 1892 came the Haut à Courroies, a large leather bag designed to carry a rider's boots and saddle. It wasn't created as a fashion accessory. It was equipment. As the automobile reshaped travel, Hermès adapted again. In 1923, it introduced the Bolide, designed for the age of the motor car and widely recognised as the first luxury leather handbag to feature a zipper.

Decades later came the Sac à Dépêches, later renamed the Kelly, and in 1984, the Birkin. They weren't departures from Hermès' history. They were its continuation.

Why It Still Matters

The remarkable thing isn't that Hermès survived the arrival of the automobile. It's that its original craft still shapes the objects it makes today. Look closely at an Hermès bag and traces of its equestrian past are everywhere. The saddle stitch is still sewn by hand. The handles, straps and hardware all reflect techniques refined in the workshop of a harness-maker, where strength mattered just as much as elegance.

The horse disappeared from everyday travel. The craftsmanship didn't. Nearly two centuries later, Hermès continues to build leather goods with the same philosophy that shaped its earliest harnesses: create something useful, build it exceptionally well, and allow time to prove its value.

Its endurance isn't built on fashion alone. It's built on a craft that never stopped evolving, even as the world around it did.

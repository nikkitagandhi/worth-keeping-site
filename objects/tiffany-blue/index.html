The Colour You're Not Allowed to Own

Tiffany chose its robin's-egg blue in 1845, then trademarked the exact shade in 1998 — as a private, custom Pantone that exists in no public swatch book.

Key takeaway

Tiffany Blue works as a brand asset because it appears almost nowhere else in nature or design — which is exactly why it was chosen.

What Actually Happened

It started as a cover, not a logo. In 1845, Charles Lewis Tiffany chose a soft robin's-egg blue for the cover of the first Blue Book, the company's annual catalogue of jewels. It was a quiet aesthetic decision. Nothing more was meant by it.

Then the colour became the box. As the box became the brand, Tiffany made a rule that still holds: the box could never be sold on its own. It could only leave the store around something purchased — given, never bought. The one object inside worth nothing on its own, and everything.

More than a century later, Tiffany did something a handful of companies have since done, but few so deliberately. In 1998, it registered the shade itself as a trademark — within the jewellery trade. Not a logo. Not a pattern. The colour. In 2001, Pantone made it a private, custom colour: 1837 Blue, named for the year Tiffany was founded. Unlike most brand colours, which use standard Pantone numbers anyone can look up, this one appears in no public swatch book. The formula is restricted. No other jeweller can use it. A colour, turned into property.

Why It Still Matters

The remarkable thing isn't that Tiffany trademarked a colour. It's that people learned to recognise the colour before they saw the logo. Today, Tiffany Blue belongs to a small group of colours that have become inseparable from the companies behind them. Christian Louboutin's red sole transformed a brushstroke of nail polish into one of fashion's most closely defended signatures. Decades earlier, the synthetic purple discovered by William Henry Perkin became the foundation of an entire dye industry, changing how colour could be produced and valued.

The jewellery changes. The collections evolve. The colour remains. Its endurance isn't simply the result of legal protection. It's the result of decades of consistency, where one carefully chosen shade became powerful enough to communicate craftsmanship, celebration and anticipation without a single word. Some things are kept, not bought.

The Louis Vuitton Trunk Was Flat for a Reason

Before Louis Vuitton, travel trunks were domed on top to shed rainwater — which also meant they couldn't be stacked. In 1858, Vuitton flattened the lid, and the modern idea of luggage was born.

Key takeaway

The trunk wasn't redesigned to look better. It was redesigned so more of them could fit on a train.

What Actually Happened

Packing used to be a profession you hired. Before Louis Vuitton became the world's most famous trunk maker, he was a layetier-emballeur—a professional packer. Wealthy families employed specialists to prepare wardrobes for long journeys, and Vuitton became so skilled at the craft that he was eventually entrusted with packing for Empress Eugénie. Watching people travel taught him something. The problem wasn't what people packed. It was what they packed it in. At the time, most trunks had domed lids. The design made perfect sense when luggage travelled on the roofs of horse-drawn carriages, where rainwater needed somewhere to run. Then travel changed. Railways and steamships moved luggage indoors, stacking it tightly inside baggage cars and cargo holds. Suddenly, a curved lid wasted valuable space. Nothing balanced on a curve. In 1858, Vuitton introduced a lightweight trunk covered in grey Trianon canvas with a flat lid. It wasn't designed to be more elegant. It was designed to be stackable. As the house grew, the plain Trianon canvas gave way to the Damier pattern in 1888 and the Monogram canvas in 1896, both helping distinguish authentic Vuitton trunks as imitation became increasingly common. The patterns became iconic. The shape came first.

Why It Still Matters

The remarkable thing isn't that the Louis Vuitton trunk became a luxury object. It's that it first became a better solution. More than 160 years later, almost every suitcase still follows the same blueprint. Whether it's hard-sided or soft, the shape remains remarkably familiar: a flat top, straight sides and square corners designed to stack efficiently, maximise space and travel well. Your suitcase is still that shape. The horse-drawn carriage disappeared. Steamships gave way to aeroplanes. Paper tickets became digital boarding passes. The way we travel changed. The shape of the luggage barely did. The canvas everyone recognises came later. The shape came first—and mattered more. It isn't the decoration that lasted. It's craftsmanship applied to a problem so completely that the world never found a better answer.

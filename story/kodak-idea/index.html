Kodak Didn't Sell Cameras — It Sold Memory.

Kodak's founder George Eastman didn't build a camera company to sell equipment — he built one to sell the idea that ordinary moments were worth keeping.

SEE THE FULL CAROUSEL ON INSTAGRAM
Key takeaway

Kodak's slogan was never really about film. "You press the button, we do the rest" was a promise that memory itself could be made effortless.

What Actually Happened

Before George Eastman, photography was work. Cameras were bulky. Glass plates had to be prepared, exposed and developed. Taking a photograph required knowledge, equipment and patience. Eastman wanted to change that. In 1888, he introduced the Kodak No. 1: a handheld camera pre-loaded with enough roll film for 100 exposures. You didn't need to understand the chemistry. You didn't even need to develop the photographs yourself. You simply pressed the button. Then you sent the camera back to Kodak. Kodak developed the film, made the prints, reloaded the camera and returned it to you. The promise appeared in the advertising almost immediately: “You press the button, we do the rest.” It wasn't just a clever slogan. It described the entire business model. Eastman wanted photography to become “as convenient as the pencil.” And it worked. Photography stopped belonging exclusively to professionals and serious amateurs. Families could photograph birthdays. Parents could record their children growing up. Travellers could bring home evidence of where they had been. The camera became less important than what happened after you pressed the button. You got to keep the moment. Kodak had not simply made photography easier. It had made remembering easier.

Why It Still Matters

The promise survived even when the technology changed. Kodak eventually became one of the defining companies of photography precisely because it understood that people weren't buying film for the sake of film. They were buying a way to keep moments. Then came the digital camera. And Kodak had invented that too. In 1975, Kodak engineer Steven Sasson built the world's first digital camera. The company went on to develop digital imaging products, including the professional Kodak DCS system in 1991 and the consumer DC40 in 1995. But digital photography eventually changed the economics of the business Kodak had built around film. The camera became a phone. The photograph became instant. And eventually, the photograph became something most of us take without thinking. Yet the original promise remains almost perfectly intact. You press the button. You keep the moment. The technology changed from film to pixels, from prints to screens, from a Kodak camera to a phone in your pocket. But the reason we take photographs never really changed. We still photograph the birthday. The journey. The person sitting across the table. The ordinary Tuesday we somehow know we will want to remember. Kodak's greatest invention may not have been a camera or a film. It was the idea that an ordinary moment was worth keeping.

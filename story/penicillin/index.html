PenicillinHow a Forgotten Petri Dish Saved Millions of Lives

In 1928, Alexander Fleming returned from holiday to find a stray mould had killed the bacteria in a petri dish he'd forgotten to clean - and modern antibiotics began by accident.

Key takeaway

Penicillin wasn't the product of a plan. It was the product of someone noticing a mistake instead of throwing it away.

What Actually Happened

The laboratory was not exactly where you would expect a medical revolution to begin. In September 1928, Alexander Fleming returned to his laboratory at St Mary's Hospital in London after a holiday. On his bench was a stack of culture plates containing Staphylococcus, bacteria Fleming had been studying.

One plate had been contaminated. A mould had grown across part of it. Normally, that would have been rubbish. But Fleming noticed something strange. Around the mould was a perfectly clear area. The bacteria had disappeared. The mould was somehow stopping them from growing.

Fleming isolated the mould and identified it as Penicillium notatum. He called the antibacterial substance it produced penicillin. The remarkable thing wasn't that mould had appeared on a laboratory plate. Contamination happened. The remarkable thing was that Fleming looked at the contamination instead of throwing it away.

He began investigating it. And what looked like a ruined experiment had revealed something entirely new: a substance produced by a living organism that could kill bacteria. But the discovery was still only a beginning. The penicillin Fleming produced was unstable and extremely difficult to purify. For years, the substance remained more scientific curiosity than medicine. The mould had done its part. The hard work was still ahead.

Why It Still Matters

It took more than a decade for that contaminated dish to become a treatment. In 1938 - 39, Howard Florey and Ernst Chain began investigating penicillin at Oxford, with Norman Heatley and others helping solve the enormous practical problems of extracting, purifying and producing it. By 1940, they had demonstrated its therapeutic potential, and in 1941 penicillin was being tested in seriously ill patients.

The world before that was brutally different. A bacterial infection that is routinely treated today could become fatal. Wounds could turn into deadly infections. There was no modern antibiotic medicine waiting on a pharmacy shelf. The amount of early penicillin was almost absurdly small. Oxford's team needed enormous quantities of culture to produce enough drug for a single case; early supplies were so precious that penicillin could even be recovered from patients' urine and reused.

Fleming found the mould. Florey, Chain, Heatley and their colleagues helped turn it into a medicine. That distinction matters because the story isn't really about an accident. The accident was the mould. The discovery was noticing it. A contaminated dish could have been washed away with everything else. Instead, someone stopped and looked.

Common Questions

Was penicillin discovered by accident?

Yes. In 1928 Alexander Fleming returned from holiday to find a stray mould had killed the bacteria in a petri dish he'd forgotten to clean.

Who discovered penicillin?

Alexander Fleming made the discovery, though it was later scientists - Howard Florey, Ernst Chain and their team - who turned it into a usable drug.

Why does the penicillin story still matter?

It shows how a forgotten, seemingly failed experiment - noticed by a curious mind - became one of the most important medical breakthroughs in history.
